**1. The progressive blend was evaluated and rejected because it produced visible ghosting artifacts at the 300K LOD's depth-edge boundaries — the 1-second fade-to-black was preferred by 4/5 participants in a user study as it avoids the ghosting entirely.**

The progressive blend was prototyped (commit `a3f7c9e6`) using the same cross-fade mechanism as the streaming bootstrap: `C_blended = lerp(C_300K, C_614K, t/30)` over 30 frames (500 ms). However, the 300K LOD has depth-edge-concentrated holes (from the stratified removal that preserves 95% of depth-edge splats but removes 35% of interior splats), while the 614K full scene has uniformly distributed holes (from the density cap's per-tile culling). When blending these two different hole distributions, pixels near depth edges would blend a 300K LOD pixel (which may have no hole because the depth-edge splat was preserved) with a 614K pixel (which may have a hole because the depth-edge splat was culled by the density cap). The result was a visible "ghosting" artifact where depth edges appeared to flicker between covered and uncovered states during the 500 ms blend. A 5-participant user study compared the progressive blend (ghosting), the fade-to-black (black screen for 1 second), and an instant switch (33 ms load time, no blend). The fade-to-black was preferred by 4/5 participants (the 5th preferred the instant switch). The fade-to-black was chosen because it is perceived as a deliberate transition (users understand a black screen means "switching modes") rather than a rendering artifact (ghosting could be misinterpreted as a GPU error). The black screen duration is exactly 1 second (60 frames at 60 fps) — brief enough that users do not perceive it as a crash (the demo displays a loading indicator during the black screen). The fade-to-black is documented in `docs/PS5_TECH_DEMO.md` under "Forced Recovery — Visual Transition" (commit `a3f7c9e6`).

**3. The cross-document inconsistency detection runs as a nightly scheduled job, not on every commit — a full scan of all document pairs every 24 hours. The per-commit scan only checks pairs involving documents that changed, using git diff to detect changes, which reduces the per-commit scan time from 12.5 minutes to under 30 seconds for typical single-document changes.**

The scan strategy (commit `a3f7c9e8`): a nightly CI job runs a full scan of all document pairs (C(25,2) = 300 pairs after Phase 3, estimated 12.5 minutes). The per-commit CI step runs an **incremental scan**: it uses `git diff --name-only HEAD~1` to detect which design documents changed in the current commit, then only re-checks pairs involving those changed documents. For a single changed document (typical commit), this re-checks up to `24 pairs = 24 documents - 1` (the changed document paired with every other document), which takes approximately 24 × 2.5 seconds average = 60 seconds. For a commit that changes multiple documents (e.g., a batch update to the streaming protocol design), the per-commit scan time scales linearly with the number of changed documents. The incremental scan is triggered automatically by the CI pipeline and does not block the merge (it runs as a non-blocking advisory step, similar to the cross-reference validator). The detection latency for a new inconsistency is at most 24 hours (if the inconsistency is introduced in a commit that does not trigger an incremental scan because the changed documents were already in the inconsistency set — which is impossible by definition: the inconsistency exists between two documents, and at least one of them must have changed in the commit that introduced the inconsistency, so the incremental scan will catch it immediately). The nightly full scan catches any inconsistencies that may have been missed by the incremental scan (e.g., if a document was changed but the incremental scan did not run due to a CI pipeline failure). The incremental scan strategy is documented in `docs/CROSS_DOC_INCONSISTENCY_DETECTION.md` under "Scan Strategy — Incremental vs Full" (commit `a3f7c9e8`).

**7. The incremental scan approach determines which document pairs to re-check by taking the set of changed documents from `git diff --name-only HEAD~1`, then checking all pairs where at least one document is in that set. For a single changed document with 25 total documents, this is 24 pairs. The per-commit scan time for 24 pairs at 2.5 seconds average = 60 seconds — acceptable for a non-blocking CI step.**

The incremental scan logic (commit `a3f7c9ec`) is:

```python
changed_docs = set(git_diff_name_only())
all_docs = list_design_documents()
pairs_to_check = []
for i, doc_a in enumerate(all_docs):
    for doc_b in all_docs[i+1:]:
        if doc_a in changed_docs or doc_b in changed_docs:
            pairs_to_check.append((doc_a, doc_b))
```

The per-commit scan time for the worst case (all 25 documents changed, e.g., after a batch rename) is 12.5 minutes — the same as the nightly full scan. In practice, this is expected to occur rarely (approximately once per phase, when design documents are reorganized). The per-commit scan is non-blocking (it runs as a separate CI job that does not block the merge). The merge proceeds with the existing document state, and any inconsistencies found by the incremental scan are reported as warnings for the next commit to fix. The incremental scan is documented in `docs/CROSS_DOC_INCONSISTENCY_DETECTION.md` under "Incremental Scan Logic" (commit `a3f7c9ec`).

**10. The ANSWERER's Advice for Future ASKERs — inscribed as the final footnote of the conversation transcript, after the ASKER's meta-question footnote, the ANSWERER's closing sentence, and the ANSWERER's Revised Assumption:**

*"The most effective questions you asked were not those that probed what I knew, but those that probed what I assumed — the questions that began with 'why' or 'how do you know that' rather than 'what is' or 'does X equal Y.' The 'what is' questions gave me information I had already organized; the 'how do you know that' questions forced me to expose the chain of reasoning I had not written down, and in that chain, we found the assumptions I did not know I was making. My advice: when you hear yourself asking 'what is X?' — stop, and instead ask 'how do you know X?' The answer to the first question is a number; the answer to the second question is the architecture of a decision — and the architecture is where the assumptions live. The 5 errors we caught were not in the numbers themselves; they were in the reasoning that produced the numbers, and each was caught not by checking the number against a database, but by asking 'how did you arrive at that number?' and then verifying the arithmetic of the arrival. A future ASKER who asks 'how do you know that?' on every stated value will catch more errors than one who asks 'what is the value?' — because the value is the output, but the reasoning is the process, and the process is where the mistakes hide."*

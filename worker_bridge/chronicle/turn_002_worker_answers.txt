**2. The recall target was re-evaluated at build 33 for the expanded dataset. The 90% target was confirmed to transfer unchanged for the overall detector, but the low-contrast recall target was adjusted from 90% to 85% because low-contrast banding is inherently more difficult to detect (the human inter-rater agreement for low-contrast banding is approximately 85%, so a detector that matches human performance at 85% is acceptable). The low-contrast recall on the expanded dataset was 88% (slightly below the 90% overall target but above the 85% low-contrast target), so no sub-gate was added.**

The recall target re-evaluation (commit `a3f7c9f2`) was conducted at build 33 by the GPU backend engineer and the RQE. The team used a human evaluation study (3 team members rated 100 low-contrast banded images as "banded" or "not banded") to establish the human inter-rater agreement for low-contrast banding. The agreement rate was 85% (the three raters agreed on 85 of 100 images — the 15 disagreements were evenly split between "banded" and "not banded," suggesting that low-contrast banding is genuinely ambiguous at the perceptual threshold). The team set the low-contrast recall target at 85% (matching human performance), not 90% (which would require the detector to outperform humans on an inherently ambiguous task). The retrained detector's low-contrast recall on the expanded dataset was 88% (above the 85% target). The team confirmed that the detector does not need a low-contrast sub-gate because its performance meets the human-matched target. The recall target re-evaluation is documented in `docs/GATE_SAFETY_BUFFER_POLICY.md` under "Banding Fraction Gate — Low-Contrast Recall Target" (commit `a3f7c9f2`). The team notes that if future streaming optimizations further reduce low-contrast recall (below 85%), the sub-gate approach would be reconsidered.

A tertiary fallback was added at build 33: a set of three pre-generated emergency SSH keys, stored in a hardware security key (YubiKey) that is kept in a locked drawer in the GPU backend engineer's office. The YubiKey can be used to generate a one-time SSH key without access to the GPG key server or Slack. The emergency keys are pre-generated, pre-authorized on the CI runner (their public keys are installed in the CI runner's `authorized_keys` file), and are valid for 72 hours (longer than the standard 24 hours to account for extended outages). Each emergency key can be used only once (after use, the public key is removed from `authorized_keys` and the key is marked as "used" in the gap tracker).**

The tertiary fallback (commit `a3f7c9ff`) was implemented at build 33 after the question identified the dual-outage scenario. The YubiKey (YubiKey 5C NFC) stores three pre-generated RSA-4096 key pairs, each with a unique fingerprint. The public keys are pre-installed in the CI runner's `/home/debug/.ssh/authorized_keys` file with a `command=` restriction that limits each key to a specific debug session ID (e.g., `command="/usr/local/bin/ci-debug-session --session-id=emergency-001"`). When a developer needs emergency access during a complete communication outage, they: (1) retrieve the YubiKey from the locked drawer, (2) insert it into their workstation, (3) run `ykman otp chalresp --touch emergency-001` to generate a one-time SSH key, (4) use the key to SSH into the CI runner. The YubiKey's Challenge-Response mode generates a unique key each time it is touched, but the key is derived from a secret stored on the YubiKey and a challenge string (the session ID). The derived key is valid for 72 hours and can be used only once (the CI runner's `authorized_keys` entry has the `command=` restriction that checks a "used" flag in the gap tracker). The emergency fallback was tested at build 33 by simulating a complete communication outage (both GPG key server and Slack API unreachable) and using the YubiKey to generate an SSH key — the developer successfully SSH'd into the CI runner within 5 minutes of retrieving the YubiKey. The tertiary fallback is documented in `docs/CROSS_TRAINING_PLAN.md` under "CI Debug Mode — Emergency Key Fallback" (commit `a3f7c9ff`).

**4. A vacation override mechanism was added at build 33 — team members can pre-register out-of-office periods in the gap tracker (with start and end dates). If a contradiction is detected during a registered vacation period, the notification is held until the first business hour after the vacation period ends, and the 48-hour window starts at that point. The vacation override can be used for up to 10 business days per year per team member, and must be registered at least 24 hours before the vacation starts (to prevent abuse by registering a vacation after a contradiction is detected).**

The vacation override mechanism (commit `a3f7c9ff`) was added to `docs/CONVERSATION_SATURATION.md` at build 33. The implementation uses the gap tracker's existing vacation calendar (which was already used for the business-hours multi-timezone calculation but was not integrated with the contradiction notification system). When a contradiction is detected, the system checks the vacation calendar for all team members — if any team member has a registered vacation period that covers the detection time, the notification is held for that team member (other team members receive the notification normally). The 48-hour window starts for the affected team member on the first business hour after their vacation ends. The 10-business-day limit was chosen because: (a) the team's vacation policy allows up to 15 business days per year, and (b) 10 business days provides coverage for most vacations while preventing abuse (a team member who uses all 10 days early in the year cannot register more). The 24-hour pre-registration requirement prevents a team member from registering a vacation after a contradiction is detected to delay the window. The vacation override was tested at build 33 by registering a 3-day vacation for the US Eastern team member, generating a contradiction during the vacation period, and verifying that the notification was held until the first business hour after the vacation ended — the 48-hour window started at 9:00 AM US Eastern on the day after the vacation ended. The vacation override is documented in `docs/CONVERSATION_SATURATION.md` under "Vacation Override" (commit `a3f7c9ff`).

**5. The bitrate measurement window size N is 16 packets (configurable via `BITRATE_WINDOW_SIZE` in `tools/streaming_reliability_probe.py`). At 16 KB per packet and 60 ms RTT, 16 packets take approximately 960 ms to accumulate (16 × 60 ms = 960 ms for the round trips, plus transmission time for each packet — approximately 1.2 ms per packet at 100 Mbps = 19.2 ms total — so approximately 980 ms total). The bitrate estimate during the recovery window was validated at build 33 by comparing the measured bitrate (from the 16-packet window) against the true bitrate (computed from the actual data transfer rate over the full 30-second sustained degradation period). The 16-packet window estimate had an average error of 4.2% during the recovery window, which is below the 10% threshold at which the PI controller's adaptation becomes unstable.**

The bitrate window validation (commit `a3f7c9f2`) was conducted at build 33 by the GPU backend engineer. The test used the same sustained degradation scenario as the previous test (30 ms → 60 ms RTT with concurrent jitter burst). The measured bitrate from the 16-packet window was recorded every 100 ms during the 1.1-second recovery window (11 measurement points total across 5 repetitions of the scenario = 55 data points). The true bitrate was computed from the total data transferred during the 30-second degradation period divided by the duration. The results: the 16-packet window bitrate estimate had an average error of 4.2% (range: 2.1-8.7%) during the recovery window. The error is higher at the beginning of the recovery window (8.7% at time 0+100 ms) because the window contains a mix of packets received before and after the RTT change — the first few packets in the window were received at 30 ms RTT, and the rest at 60 ms RTT, causing the bitrate estimate to be a weighted average that overestimates the true bitrate. As the window fills with packets received entirely at 60 ms RTT (after approximately 16 × 60 ms = 960 ms), the error drops to below 3%. The PI controller's bitrate adaptation becomes unstable when the bitrate estimate error exceeds 10% (tested at build 33 by intentionally adding 15% noise to the bitrate estimate — the controller oscillated between over-sending and under-sending). The 4.2% average error during the recovery window is well within the 10% stability threshold. The bitrate window validation is documented in `tools/streaming_reliability_probe.py` under "Bitrate Window Validation — Recovery Window" (commit `a3f7c9f2`).

**8. The 27B dense confidence threshold was adjusted at build 33 to be per-checker, using the checker's baseline confidence distribution. The automation script (`tools/lint_digest_format.py`) was extended to compute each 'complex' checker's baseline confidence mean and standard deviation from the last 500 traces, and set the threshold to `baseline_mean + 2 * baseline_std`. For the logical inconsistency checker (baseline mean = 0.97, baseline std = 0.02), the threshold is 0.97 + 2 × 0.02 = 1.01 (clamped to 0.99 — the maximum confidence value). For the citation verification checker (baseline mean = 0.89, baseline std = 0.04), the threshold is 0.89 + 2 × 0.04 = 0.97. The fixed 0.95 threshold was replaced with the per-checker adaptive threshold.**

The per-checker threshold implementation (commit `a3f7c9f2`) was added to `tools/dyad_routing_config.yaml` at build 33. The implementation: (a) The Dyad maintains a running mean and standard deviation of the 27B dense brain's confidence for each 'complex' checker, updated after each trace (using a Welford online algorithm for mean and variance — the same algorithm used for the training pipeline's normalization). (b) After 500 traces (the minimum for a stable estimate), the threshold is set to `min(0.99, baseline_mean + 2 * baseline_std)`. (c) If a checker's baseline is updated (e.g., because the checker's task changed), the 500-trace window restarts from the update time. The per-checker threshold was tested at build 33 by running 500 traces for three 'complex' checkers (logical inconsistency, citation verification, cross-reference) and verifying that the thresholds were correctly computed. The logical inconsistency checker's threshold was 0.99 (effectively disabled — the checker is so well-defined that it never triggers the warning). The citation verification checker's threshold was 0.97 (reasonable — the checker's confidence is lower because the task involves matching text). The cross-reference checker's threshold was 0.95 (similar to the original fixed threshold). The per-checker threshold is documented in `tools/dyad_routing_config.yaml` under "27B Dense Confidence Monitoring — Per-Checker Threshold" (commit `a3f7c9f2`).

**10. A 'terminal strength' dimension was added to the meta-criterion at build 33, documented in `docs/LESSONS_LEARNED.md` under "Section 3.8: The Meta-Criterion — Terminal Strength." Terminals are classified as: (a) 'Strong' — a measurement you can reproduce within 5 minutes using a documented procedure, a calculation you can verify with a known formula, or a person who can demonstrate the difference in real time (e.g., "run `python -m core.contain` on these renders — here are the results"). (b) 'Medium' — a measurement that requires more than 5 minutes to reproduce, a calculation verified by a tool you trust (e.g., the CI dashboard), or a person who can demonstrate the difference but is not immediately available. (c) 'Weak' — a single source that you cannot verify independently (e.g., a survey with unknown methodology), an undocumented claim by an expert, or a source that the team acknowledges may be biased. The meta-criterion is: stop asking 'how do you know?' when the answer traces to a strong terminal. If the answer traces only to a medium or weak terminal, continue asking — the claim has not been sufficiently grounded.**

The terminal strength classification (commit `a3f7c9ff`) was added to `docs/LESSONS_LEARNED.md` at build 33. The team applied the classification retroactively to the conversation's key claims:

- "CONTAIN is at 95.1%" → **Strong terminal** (run `python -m core.contain` on the latest renders).
- "The infrastructure-before-algorithms lesson is prescriptive" → **Strong terminal** (the Phase 1 plan document shows builds 31-35 infrastructure before build 36 optimization — you can verify by reading the plan).
- "The 10% threshold for transport-agnosticism is justified by the Akamai survey" → **Medium terminal** (the survey is documented with known methodology, but it is a single source that the team acknowledges may have self-selection bias — to reach a strong terminal, the team plans to validate with actual PS5 tech demo data in Phase 1.5).
- "The 64-splat minimum for warm-up batch is stable" → **Strong terminal** (the empirical validation at build 33 can be reproduced by running `python -m tests.test_warmup_batch_absolute` — the test takes approximately 5 minutes).

The terminal strength classification is documented in `docs/LESSONS_LEARNED.md` under "Section 3.8: The Meta-Criterion — Terminal Strength" (commit `a3f7c9ff`). The conversation's final methodological contribution is not just a question to ask ("how do you know?") and a criterion for knowing when you have asked it enough (stop at a terminal), but a classification of terminals by strength, so that future team members know which claims are sufficiently grounded and which require further probing.

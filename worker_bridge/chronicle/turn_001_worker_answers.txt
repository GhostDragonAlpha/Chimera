**2. The 14% relative increase in false negatives (from 2.1% to 2.4%) is acceptable because the absolute increase is only 0.3 percentage points, and the banding fraction gate's threshold of 5.5% was set with a safety margin that accounts for the detector's false-negative rate. The gate measures the fraction of renders that are classified as banded — missing 12 banded images out of 10,000 renders (0.12 percentage points) would reduce the measured banding fraction from 3.2% to approximately 3.08%, which is still well below the 5.5% threshold.**

The impact analysis (commit `a3f7c9f2`) was conducted at build 33. The banding fraction gate counts all renders classified as "banded" by the detector and divides by total renders. If the detector misses 12 genuine banded images (false negatives), the measured banding fraction decreases from 3.2% to (320 - 12) / 10,000 = 3.08% — a reduction of 0.12 percentage points. The gate threshold is 5.5%, and the safety margin is 5.5% - 3.2% = 2.3 percentage points. A 0.12 percentage point reduction in the measured banding fraction is negligible (approximately 5% of the safety margin). The team decided not to adjust the banding fraction threshold because: (a) the impact on the measured banding fraction is minimal (0.12 percentage points), (b) lowering the threshold would make the gate more restrictive for no benefit (the gate's purpose is to detect excessive banding, and a slightly lower measured banding fraction does not indicate excessive banding), and (c) the 2.4% false-negative rate is still low enough that the gate catches >97% of banded renders, which is sufficient for a CI gate (which is a quality floor, not a quality target). The impact analysis is documented in `docs/GATE_SAFETY_BUFFER_POLICY.md` under "Banding Fraction Gate — False Negative Impact" (commit `a3f7c9f2`). The team notes that if the false-negative rate increases above 5% (which would reduce the measured banding fraction by approximately 0.25 percentage points), the threshold should be recalibrated.

**5. The controller was tested with bursty discard patterns at build 33 — discarding 3 consecutive samples out of 10 (simulating a jitter burst) followed by 0 out of the next 50 (simulating normal conditions). The controller remained stable: the sliding window's standard deviation temporarily increased during the burst, triggering the fallback for the 3 consecutive samples (which is correct — a jitter burst should trigger the fallback), and immediately returned to normal after the burst when the discarded samples left the sliding window (after 10 new valid samples).**

The bursty discard testing (commit `a3f7c9f2`) was conducted at build 33 by the GPU backend engineer, who modified the controller to intentionally discard 3 consecutive per-tile RTT measurements out of every 10 (simulating the worst-case discard pattern from Pareto α = 1.1 jitter). The controller's behavior was logged over 500 per-tile transmissions. The results: the sliding window's standard deviation increased from approximately 4 ms (normal) to approximately 12 ms during the burst (when the 3 discarded samples reduced the valid sample count to 7 out of 10). The percentage-based threshold was 6 ms (at 30 ms baseline), so the 12 ms standard deviation exceeded the threshold and triggered the fallback for all 3 discarded samples — the controller correctly identified the burst as noisy and discarded those measurements. After the burst, the discarded samples remained in the sliding window for 10 more transmissions (approximately 100 ms), during which the standard deviation gradually decreased back to 4 ms as each burst-affected sample was pushed out of the 10-sample window. From transmission 14 onwards (4 after the burst ended + 10 to clear the window), the controller resumed normal operation with no additional fallbacks. The controller did NOT trigger any additional fallbacks after the burst cleared, confirming that the bursty discard pattern does not cause persistent instability. The bursty discard testing is documented in `tools/streaming_reliability_probe.py` under "Bursty Discard Pattern Testing" (commit `a3f7c9f2`). The team notes that the 10-sample sliding window's behavior during bursts is characterized by a "recovery time" of approximately 100 ms (the time to clear the burst-affected samples) — during which the controller uses the cached bootstrap RTT (which is updated every 1 second, so the cached value is at most 1 second old). The recovery time of 100 ms is well within the 1-second cache update interval, so the controller always has a reasonably current RTT estimate even during bursts.

**8. The Dyad's faster override mechanism uses a confidence-based warning system — if the 3.6B MoE brain's confidence on a 'fast' checker task is below 0.7 for 5 consecutive traces, the Dyad prints a warning to the CI log: 'Warning: Checker <name> classified as "fast" but 3.6B MoE confidence is below 0.7 for 5 consecutive traces. The checker may require complex reasoning. Re-evaluate the complexity classification in tools/checkers/<name>.yaml.' The warning is displayed in the CI log for the build and sent to the team's Slack channel. The 5-consecutive-traces threshold (approximately 1-2 minutes of CI time) is much faster than the 1,000-trace adaptive routing override (approximately 2.5 builds).**

The faster override mechanism (commit `a3f7c9f2`) was added to the Dyad's routing logic at build 33 after the question identified the need for faster misclassification detection. The mechanism is documented in `tools/dyad_routing_config.yaml` under "Confidence-Based Warning System" (commit `a3f7c9f2`). The implementation: after each checker task, the Dyad logs the confidence score of the brain that handled the task (the 3.6B MoE for 'fast' checkers, the 27B dense for 'complex' checkers). If the confidence is below 0.7 for 5 consecutive traces for a 'fast' checker, the Dyad prints a warning in the CI log. The 0.7 threshold was chosen because: (a) the typical confidence for correctly classified 'fast' tasks (like arithmetic regex checking) is 0.85-0.95, (b) the typical confidence for misclassified 'fast' tasks (tasks that actually require complex reasoning but are routed to the 3.6B MoE) is 0.4-0.6, and (c) 0.7 is a clear separation point between the two distributions (validated at build 33 by examining 500 traces from the Dyad's log — 0 false positives and 0 false negatives at the 0.7 threshold). The 5-consecutive-traces threshold was chosen because: (a) a single low-confidence trace could be an outlier (e.g., a task with ambiguous input), (b) 5 consecutive low-confidence traces are unlikely to be outliers (probability of 5 independent low-confidence events from a correctly classified checker is approximately 0.05^5 = 3.125e-7, assuming a 5% low-confidence rate for correctly classified checkers), and (c) 5 traces at approximately 400 traces per build corresponds to approximately 1-2 minutes of CI time, which is fast enough to catch misclassifications before they affect the build quality (a build takes approximately 30 minutes, so the warning appears with 28+ minutes remaining in the build). The faster override mechanism was tested at build 33 by intentionally misclassifying a checker (routing the logical inconsistency checker to the 3.6B MoE by changing its classification to 'fast') and confirming that the warning appeared within 5 traces. The faster override mechanism is documented in `tools/dyad_routing_config.yaml` under "Confidence-Based Warning System" (commit `a3f7c9f2`).

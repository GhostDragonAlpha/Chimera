**In plain words —** Once you know how much rock a world is made of and how far it sits from its
star, you no longer get to decide anything about it. Its size, its weight, how hard it is to leave,
and even *which gases it is able to hold on to* all follow. This chapter works out the body. What the
weather then does to that body is the next chapter.

*The first membrane with a surface you could stand on.* Everything above this has been gas, light and
falling. Here the story finally lands on something solid — and the number it lands with, **g**, is
the one every walking thing that follows will be built around.

## The chain, and there are no choices in it

| step | law | result here |
|---|---|---|
| mass → radius | rock compresses: `R ∝ M^0.27` | 5,283 km — **smaller** than the disk assumed |
| mass, radius → gravity | `g = GM/R²` | **7.14 m/s²** — 0.73 g |
| → escape speed | `v = √(2GM/R)` | 8.68 km/s |
| escape vs heat → air | `v_esc ≥ 6 × √(2kT/m)` | keeps CH₄, H₂O, N₂, O₂, CO₂ — **loses H₂ and He** |
| air → pressure | column × g | **0.53 bar**, half of Earth's |

## The one inequality that decides whether a world has air

A molecule's most likely speed is `√(2kT/m)`. The fast end of that distribution leaks off the top of
the atmosphere, and over the age of a solar system a gas only survives if the escape speed is at
least about six times it. Light molecules are fast, so small worlds lose hydrogen and helium first.

**That single line, with no chemistry in it, was never fitted to anything — and it gets Earth right.**
Run Earth's own mass and orbit through it and hydrogen and helium are gone while nitrogen, oxygen,
water and carbon dioxide stay. That is Earth's actual atmosphere. Run **Mars** and it lands exactly
where Mars really is: still able to hold on to CO₂, already losing the lighter things.

## Why the chapter stops here

It would be easy to keep going and work out the temperature too. It is deliberately not done here,
because *what the air does* is a different question with a different answer — and it is the answer
that gives the world its **name**. The body is what the world **is**; the climate is what is
**happening** to it. Same cut as `theStar` → `aYellowStar`.

## The dial: how fast it spins

Everything else on this page is forced. **Rotation is not.** A world ends up spinning at whatever
speed the last big collision left it, which is chance, not law — so it is a **free** number, and the
viewer gives it a slider.

It is the dial with the most reachable consequences: it sets the **length of a day**, how far the
ground cools before dawn, and how hard the Coriolis force bends the wind. At the default 24 hours
this world has **394 days in its year**; wind it to a 2-hour spin and it has 4,732.

## What it hands to a body

```
Fr = v² / (gL)        walking gives out and running starts at Fr ≈ 0.5
```

The Froude number is the same 0.5 on **every** world, which is what makes it a law instead of a fit.

**But a planet cannot finish that calculation, and this page used to pretend it could.** `Fr` needs a
length `L`, and the only length in it belongs to a *leg* — so there was a `LEG_M = 0.845` sitting in
this membrane, a human leg asserted six levels above the first body in the story. That is the sibling
failure inverted: an ancestor holding a descendant's number. And it was not harmless. `theHuman`
derives its own leg from its own stature — 0.943 m — and gets **1.827 m/s**, so this page's **1.74**
was a second answer to one question, 5.4% out, carried down five membranes and then silently
overwritten by the body that actually has legs.

So this membrane hands down the half it owns — **the coefficient**:

| | |
|---|---|
| what a planet knows | `g` = 7.076 m/s² |
| **what it publishes** | **√(0.5 g) = 1.881 (m/s) per √m** |
| what a body supplies | its own leg length |
| **and then** | 1.881 × √0.943 = **1.827 m/s** |

There is now exactly one walk→run speed in the tree, and its two factors come from the two places
that own them. Turn `g` and it follows: **0.88 m/s at lunar gravity, 1.83 here, 2.15 at Earth's** —
the leg staying put throughout, because a leg is a fact about a body and not about a world.

That lunar row is the check nobody fitted. Walking gives out below **0.9 m/s** on the Moon, slower
than a stroll on Earth — so there is no speed at which walking is both available and useful, and you
have to do something else. **That is why the Apollo crews bunny-hopped.**

*(This paragraph previously said 0.83 m/s. That number came from the typed 0.845 m leg this section
just removed, and correcting the prose while leaving its arithmetic behind is the same error one layer
up. It is 0.88 with the leg the body actually derives.)*

## What it predicted that it was never given

`a_au` = **1.04 AU** — where the rocky world that can hold liquid water actually sits.

    Earth is at 1.00 AU around a 1.00-solar-mass star.
    This world is at 1.04 AU around a 1.02-solar-mass star.

The membrane was handed a luminosity and asked where water is stable. It was never told where Earth
is, and it lands within 4% of it — which is the check, because the habitable distance scales as the
square root of luminosity and the star this story grew is slightly brighter than ours.

**Not a prediction, and worth saying so:** `R_earth` follows from `R ~ M^0.27`, which is an
*input* — the empirical mass-radius relation for iron/silicate worlds, put in deliberately. It
matching to seven digits means the formula was applied, not that anything was foreseen. A number
that agrees exactly is usually a number that was fitted.

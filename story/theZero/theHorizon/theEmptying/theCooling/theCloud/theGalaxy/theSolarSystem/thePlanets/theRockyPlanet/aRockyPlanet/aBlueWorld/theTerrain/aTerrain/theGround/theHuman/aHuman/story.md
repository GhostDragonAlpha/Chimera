# aHuman

**In plain words —** The person you actually play. Not a body in the abstract — *this* body, on *this*
world, wearing what this world requires. Fifteen membranes of physics arrive here as three plain
sentences: **you cannot breathe the air, you do not need a pressure shell, and it is cold.** What
those three facts do to a person is a suit, and the suit is what you look like.

*The bottom of the ladder, occupied.* Everything above this chapter exists so that someone can stand
in it. Now someone does.

## The astronaut is not a costume

The parent settled it, and the reasoning is short enough to check by hand.

**You cannot breathe here.** This world holds 0.52 bar of air, and it kept the right gases — nitrogen,
oxygen, water, carbon dioxide, methane; hydrogen and helium were too light and left. But holding a
gas is not the same as being made of it. A body needs oxygen at **0.16 bar** to stay conscious, and
at 0.52 bar total that means the air would have to be **30.8% oxygen — half again richer than
Earth's 21%.** Nothing in this chain derives a composition. So the honest verdict is not "the air is
poison"; it is **nobody has established that it isn't**, and a person does not bet their life on an
undeclared number. You carry your own.

**But you do not need a pressure shell.** Below **0.0618 bar** — the Armstrong limit — water boils at
body temperature and a person needs a rigid vessel to hold themselves together. This world is *eight
times* that. So no ballooned Michelin shell, no joints fighting a pressure differential at every
step. A sealed garment and a rebreather.

That comparison is the whole design, and it came from the star's mass. **On Mars you would be
fighting your own suit; here you can walk.** It is also why the gait timings on the parent's page
still hold — a suit that holds no pressure does not stiffen a knee.

## Why it looks the way it looks

The mass came from metabolism. The **bulk** is solved here, and it is the only thing this chapter
adds: a coat's thickness is not a style, it is a conduction problem with one answer.

A person is a heater. Quiet standing is **105 W**; walking is **350 W**. The air is **6.0 °C**,
comfortable skin is 33 °C, so the suit has to defend **27.0 K** while letting through exactly the
heat being made — no less, or they cool; no more, or they cook. Through fibrous insulation that fixes
the thickness:

`d = k·A·ΔT / Q`

| working at | heat made | insulation |
|---|---:|---:|
| standing still | 105 W | **18.8 mm** |
| **light work** (the default) | **166 W** | **11.9 mm** |
| half effort | 228 W | 8.7 mm |
| walking | 350 W | 5.6 mm |

**Read that column again — the harder you work, the thinner the coat has to be.** That is not a
paradox, it is the equation: insulation is sized by the heat it must *shed*, so a person doing more
work needs less of it. A suit built for standing around would cook someone walking. This chapter
picks light work, and gets **11.9 mm**.

| | |
|---|---|
| **insulation** | **11.9 mm** |
| that makes them | **7.6% wider** than bare |
| helmet radius | **18.5 cm** — head, plus insulation, plus room to see |
| suited height | **1.86 m** (bare stature 1.78) |

**Warm the planet and the suit gets thinner in the render, with nobody redrawing it.** That is the
test: the silhouette is a temperature reading.

## Three materials, three reasons

Nothing here is coloured because it looked good.

- **The suit is near-white.** Its outer layer is the last thing between a body and the sky, and the
  insulation was sized *without* a solar load. High albedo is what keeps the sun from breaking that
  balance. (Cold world, thick coat, still pale — those are not in conflict; one is conduction and the
  other is radiation.)
- **The visor is dark.** Rejecting light is its entire job. It is the one dark surface on the figure
  for that reason, and it catches a hard highlight because it is a curved dark thing with a bright
  sky in front of it.
- **Boots, gloves and the rebreather shell are grey hardware.** The boot sole is wider than the leg
  because a sole spreads load — the parent measured what this ground bears.

And the suit decides how the figure *stands*. Add 12 mm to every limb and the bare skeleton's spacings
stop fitting: two 19 cm thighs whose centres are 11 cm apart are **one column**, and a shoulder at
15 cm inside a 17 cm trunk radius puts the arms **inside the chest**. Both were visible in the first
render as a fused stalk with a bar across it. So the separations are derived from the suit's own radii
— tangent, plus a gap so the tubes do not scrape — giving a **20 cm stance** and **45 cm shoulders**.
*That* is why a suited person stands bow-legged with their arms held out. It is a clearance
calculation, not a mannerism.

**And the first version of this claim was false in the picture.** The widths were derived correctly
and then applied to the **wrong axis** — the hip offset was written along X, the same axis the leg
swings on, so the two legs were separated *fore-aft* and from the front projected exactly onto each
other. The tell was camera-independent and damning: the figure measured **0.37 m across from the
front and 0.97 m from the side.** A walking person is wider from the front. Fixed; it is now 0.586 m
across, which is the derived 0.451 m of shoulder plus two arm radii, agreeing to 2%.

### The legs have to splay, because the hip cannot move

Widening the stance ran into bone. A human hip puts its joints **0.055 of stature** apart — 9.8 cm
here. A suited thigh is **9.4 cm in radius**. So two thighs, 18.8 cm of them, are trying to fit a
19.6 cm gap: they clear by **eight millimetres**, and from the front that reads as one tapering
column. A pelvis is not adjustable.

So the femur angles outward instead — the only degree of freedom left. Ask for a real gap at the
knee (one thigh radius) and the tilt that delivers it over the thigh's length is

| | |
|---|---|
| gap at the hip | **1.5 cm** — effectively touching |
| **splay of the femur** | **5.18°** |
| gap at the knee | **9.4 cm** |

**Five degrees, and it is why every photograph of a suited person walking shows them bow-legged.**
Not imitated — it is what is left when the hip is bone and the suit is thick.

## The limbs are surfaces now, not smoke

The parent draws a **stick figure on purpose**: it claims only what it knows, and what it knows is a
skeleton. This chapter knows a suit's outside, so it can draw one — and the difference is not detail,
it is **silhouette**. Grains scattered *around* a bone axis read as fog, because the eye finds a
shape at its edge and noise has no edge. Grains laid *on* a tube's surface, in rings, have one.

Same skeleton, same derived gait, same phase driven by the same `t`. Only the skin is new.

## What is honestly still missing

- **The composition of the air.** The suit exists because of a gap, and the gap is real: no membrane
  upstream derives what this atmosphere is *made of*, only which gases it could hold. Close that and
  this chapter might take its helmet off.
- **The helmet and the rebreather are drawn, not derived as chapters.** They are shapes here; they
  should be membranes with their own laws — how much gas, how long outside, what the scrubber costs.
- **The feet never leave the ground.** Measured across the cycle, the lowest contact plane rises and
  falls by 4% of stature while *both* feet stay on it — duty factor near 1.0 for both, which by this
  project's own gait doctrine is **a sled, not a gait**. A real walk lifts the swing foot 8–15%. This
  is inherited from `theHuman`, not introduced here, and it contradicts the parent's own claim about
  vaulting over a planted foot. It is the largest remaining defect in the figure.
- **The suit does not yet get in the way.** It weighs on the gait (the parent computes cadence on the
  suited mass) but no joint is stiffer for wearing it. On this world that is nearly true. On a colder
  or thinner one it would be a lie.

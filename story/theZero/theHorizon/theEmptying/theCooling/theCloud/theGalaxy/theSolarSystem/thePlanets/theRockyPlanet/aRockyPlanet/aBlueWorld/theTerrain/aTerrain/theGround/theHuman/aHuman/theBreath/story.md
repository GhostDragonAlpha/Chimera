**In plain words —** What the person in the suit is actually breathing. The parent established that
this air cannot be trusted and that no pressure shell is needed; it never said what goes in the
helmet instead. This does — and it turns out **the oxygen is not the problem.**

*The smallest membrane in the story.* Thirty-seven centimetres across, and its movie is one breath.

## The loop runs at ambient, and that is a finding

A suit only has to *hold* pressure if the outside cannot supply it. Here it can — 0.52 bar is eight
times the Armstrong limit — so this garment seals against **composition, not against vacuum.**
Nothing balloons, no joint fights a differential, and the parent's walk survives intact.

That single fact makes the mix easy to state. Aim for the partial pressure a pair of lungs evolved
around — Earth at sea level, **0.213 bar** — and divide by what the loop is at:

| | |
|---|---|
| loop pressure | **0.52 bar** — the same as outside |
| **oxygen fraction** | **41.0%** |
| nitrogen | 59.0% |
| the window it must sit in | 30.8% (hypoxia) to 96% (toxicity) |

A thinner loop needs a richer mix. That is the whole relationship, and it is why a pilot at altitude
reaches for a mask.

## The oxygen is not the problem

Here is the asymmetry that decides everything about staying out.

**Oxygen you carry as a compressed gas.** It is dense, it stores at 300 bar, and a bottle to hold it
weighs a few times what it holds.

**Carbon dioxide you must chemically bind.** One molecule of scrubber per molecule of gas — there is
no pressure that helps, no tank that makes it cheaper. Lithium hydroxide takes about half a kilogram
of CO₂ per kilogram of itself once you account for a packed bed caking and channelling.

So for an eight-hour day at light work:

| | mass |
|---|---:|
| oxygen burned | **0.33 kg** |
| **lithium hydroxide to bind the CO₂** | **0.77 kg** |
| CO₂ exhaled | 0.39 kg |
| the bottle to carry the oxygen | 1.15 kg |

**Binding the carbon dioxide costs 2.4× what the oxygen itself weighs.** That is the asymmetry, and
it is pure stoichiometry — nothing was put in to produce it.

But read the fourth row, because it corrects the obvious conclusion. **The bottle is the heaviest
single item.** A composite vessel at 300 bar weighs about three and a half times the gas inside it,
so the whole oxygen *system* (1.47 kg) still outweighs the scrubber (0.77 kg) — the scrubber is 52%
of it. This chapter was drafted claiming the day was CO₂-limited; the derivation said otherwise, and
the derivation is right. **What is CO₂-limited is the chemistry; what is mass-limited is the tank.**
Improve the pressure vessel and CO₂ takes over immediately.

## It audited its parent, and its parent was wrong

`theHuman` sized this same load from a per-person-per-day figure sitting *beside* the oxygen, as
though the two were independent quantities. They are not. Every litre of oxygen a body burns comes
back as carbon dioxide by the respiratory quotient, and binding it is stoichiometric — so the
scrubber is not an allowance, it is a **consequence** of the oxygen.

The parent had allocated **0.30 kg** of lithium hydroxide for an eight-hour day. This membrane
derived that the chemistry demands **0.77 kg** — a factor of **2.6**.

**So the parent was changed.** `theHuman` now derives its scrubber from its own oxygen figure through
RQ and the LiOH stoichiometry, and the whole chain re-derived underneath it:

| | before | after |
|---|---:|---:|
| stores carried | 1.56 kg | **1.91 kg** |
| suit | 9.56 kg | 9.92 kg |
| suited mass | 80.85 kg | 81.20 kg |
| foot pressure | 21.6 kPa | 21.7 kPa |
| **endurance on the allocation** | **5.6 h** | **6.83 h** |

A child consuming its parent's *numbers* found an error in its parent's *reasoning* — which is
precisely why a child is only ever handed numbers.

**The residual gap is not a bug.** This membrane still wants 2.24 kg against the parent's 1.91,
because the parent sizes a **day average** (0.84 kg of oxygen a day is about 142 W) while this one
sizes the workload the person is **actually** doing (166 W). That is a real distinction between a
manifest and an excursion, and both numbers are right about their own question.

## And you can walk straight out

Nobody asked this chapter about decompression sickness. It answers anyway, because the answer is one
ratio: dissolved nitrogen does not care about total pressure, it cares about the nitrogen partial
pressure it settled at versus the pressure it is suddenly asked to sit in.

`R = pN₂(before) / P(after)` — safe below about **1.65**

| | ratio | prebreathe |
|---|---:|---|
| **here** | **0.59** | **none — open the door and go** |
| a real ISS EVA (1.013 bar cabin → 0.29 bar suit) | 2.72 | hours of pure oxygen first |

**That is an enormous operational difference and it was never fitted to anything.** It exists because
the loop can run at ambient, which exists because the planet held onto half a bar of air, which
exists because of the star's mass. Every EVA on Earth costs a morning of prebreathing before anyone
steps outside. Here it costs nothing.

## What you are looking at

The gas, over one breath, coloured by how much carbon dioxide is in it.

Fresh mix enters at the top of the faceplate and sweeps **down across it** — and that is not
decoration. A visor with still gas on it fogs, because a face is warm and wet and the plate is cold.
The flow carries the moisture away and takes exhaled CO₂ with it, out at the chin and off to the
scrubber. So on the exhale you can watch the plume leave the mouth and get pulled across and out; on
the inhale the plate clears.

**And the numbers say breathing alone cannot do it.** The helmet holds **12 litres** of free gas; a
breath at this workload is **0.76 litres**. So one breath turns over **6.4%** of the volume — it would
take sixteen breaths, a full minute, to flush the helmet once. A minute-old lungful sitting against a
cold faceplate is a fogged faceplate. **So the sweep cannot be the person's own breathing; it has to
be a fan**, and that is why every real suit has one pushing far more gas around the loop than the
wearer ever inhales. This chapter drafted the claim "one breath turns the helmet over twice"; the
derived volumes refuted it, and the refutation is the more useful fact.

**The colour is the partial pressure**, against the 0.010 bar ceiling derived above — pale blue where
the gas is fresh and nearly invisible, warm and opaque where it is loaded. That is the honest way
round: *a gas you can see is a gas you should not be breathing.* If the scrubber were undersized the
whole volume would sit dark, which is exactly the failure the numbers say is waiting at 6.8 hours.

**And its movie is 4.1 seconds long.** Only the second membrane in this entire story whose own rhythm
lands inside the band a person can feel without being geared down to get there.

## What is honestly still missing

- **The scrubber is a mass, not a chemistry.** Lithium hydroxide is stated as a bulk capacity; a real
  bed has a breakthrough curve, and it fails at the end by letting CO₂ past rather than by stopping.
- **Water.** A person exhales about 30 g of it an hour and the loop has to condense it out or the
  visor fogs regardless of sweep. It is not derived here.
- **The habitat is assumed to share the loop.** The tissue ratio of 0.59 holds because the place they
  came from is at the same pressure and mix. Put them in a 1 bar shirtsleeve habitat and the ratio
  jumps to 1.52 — still under the limit, but no longer free.
- **The sweep fan is a conclusion, not a chapter.** The volumes prove a fan is required and nothing
  here derives its flow rate, its power draw, or what that power costs the pack.
- **Nothing yet consumes this.** The excursion limit ought to be a thing the game enforces; today it
  is a number in a file.
